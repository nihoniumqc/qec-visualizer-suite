Basics of quantum computing & QEC

A five-minute run-up before the tools — what a qubit is, why it breaks, and how error correction fights back. Skip it if you already live here.

01 — The unit

A qubit isn't a bit that's just "maybe"

A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit is a point on the Bloch sphere — any state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with |α|² + |β|² = 1. The poles are the classical states; everywhere else is genuinely quantum.

That continuous freedom is the whole source of quantum advantage — and the whole reason error correction is hard. There's no discrete "flip" to simply re-measure and fix.

02 — The resource

Superposition & entanglement

Superposition lets one qubit carry a weighted blend of |0⟩ and |1⟩ simultaneously. Entangle several qubits and their joint state can't be written as separate individual states at all — measuring one instantly constrains the others.

QEC codes are built entirely out of entanglement: a logical qubit is smeared redundantly across many physical qubits, so no single physical error can read out — or destroy — the encoded information.

03 — The problem

Decoherence doesn't wait for you

Qubits leak into their environment constantly. Bit flips (X), phase flips (Z), and their combination (Y) accumulate at rates measured in microseconds — long before most useful algorithms finish running.

Worse: you can't just measure a qubit to check if it flipped. Measurement collapses the superposition itself. QEC has to detect errors without ever learning the state it's protecting.

04 — The trick

Measure the error, not the data

Stabilizer codes measure joint parity operators (stabilizers) across groups of data qubits using ancilla qubits — without ever measuring the data qubits themselves. A flipped ancilla outcome (a "syndrome") flags that an error occurred nearby, without revealing what the logical state actually is.

Read syndromes round after round and you get a defect pattern in space and time — exactly what a decoder like MWPM is built to interpret.

05 — The workhorse

The surface code

Data and ancilla qubits alternate on a 2D lattice. Each ancilla stabilizes its neighboring data qubits; syndromes light up on a checkerboard pattern whenever an error occurs. It's local, planar, and matches real superconducting and neutral-atom hardware layouts — which is why it's the leading candidate for fault tolerance today.

Every tool in the suite below is a different lens on this same lattice: how errors move through it, how decoders read it, and how logical gates are performed on it.

Why this exists

QEC is one of the most consequential problems in science right now — and almost none of it was visible outside a handful of research groups. That felt wrong.

These four tools exist to close that gap: free, interactive, and built directly from the algorithms running in real labs today.
